<span class="newthought">XKCD has been part of my life since I was at University</span>, sometime after I started studying engineering but before I started studying law. 

For anyone that hasn't heard of it, it's a ["webcomic of romance, sarcasm, math and language"](https://xkcd.com) with an unpronounceable name. The author, Randall Munroe, was a roboticist and leans into the geekier side of life: a lot of the humour is observational joking about how different domains interact, and I've learned a lot over the years reading it. Now you can too![^explain]

[^explain]: It crosses so many fields that the jokes need explaining a lot. So much that it's had its own fan-written wiki [explainXKCD](https://explainxkcd.com) since around 2012!

![Today's Lucky Ten Thousand](https://imgs.xkcd.com/comics/ten_thousand.png)

<span class="newthought">Recently my kids became two of that day's lucky ten thousand,</span> when they caught me reading it and enjoyed some frames - it turns out to be a good way for them to learn too and share the joke. It helps that they're into science and engineering. 

Unfortunately not every comic is kid-friendly. Partly there are the normal things like the occasional swearword, but there are also comics that depend on the zeitgeist of the moment; or an adult's daily experience over years; or assume knowledge of concepts that are way beyond my power to explain them, like calculus. There are also some cartoons that are a bit too deep or sad. 

How to find a way to let kids experience the fun? My solution is to simply pick the cartoons that (a) I think the kids can already understand, or (b) that depend on a topic that I can explain easily without the joke being ruined. If the topic can be explained and the joke still lands as funny afterwards, that's a double win in my book.

Everyone knows that explaining the joke is a good way to ruin it, but xkcd is a bit different for a couple of reasons. First, it's based on breadth of knowledge that very few people have anyway, and that makes it a good and fun way to learn about new concepts. Second, if the explainxkcd Wiki is anything to go by, there's a large population of people out there who routinely want the joke explained anyway. 

<span class="newthought">After going through half the comics,</span> about one third of the comics are kid-friendly by my heuristic.[^percent] 

[^percent]: More precisely: 619 out of 1794 or about 34.4%

I've put them up at [tris.sherliker.net/xkcd](https://tris.sherliker.net/xkcd) and you can see them here: 

{%- include "xkcd-panel.njk" -%}

Now, the kids can freely scroll through and get a full experience without me over their shoulder, and we can share the fun of discovering new things together too.

## Use of AI

I didn't use an LLM to make the selection, or to scrape Randall's site. But I did use it to make a neat UI tool to help me make decisions for each comic with a Y/N keyboard press (shown below), and to make a short script to download the image assets once after I selected them.

## Notes of topics I seem to find bad and good

Here are some topics that seem to fare well and poorly on my heuristic.

### Bad

- politics
- Vaccines
- Vaccine politics
- Office politics
- The Superbowl and other US culture
- Mental health problems
- Censorship
- Old movies
- Disease
- 90s internet conventions
- Calculus
- The Demon Core tragedy
- Observational humour that depends on overlapping terminology between three scientific disciplines
- Etymology
- Sports brackets
- The international phonetic alphabet
- How mobile phones have developed over the past two decades
- Cable connections
- Two-factor authentication
- Star Trek

### Good

- Syllogisms
- Space
- Unit conversions
- Physics and Chemistry topics other than quantum mechanics
- Measurement error 
- Scientific abstractions
- Dinosaurs
- Things that seem like they shouldn't work, but they do
- Observational humour that doesn't depend on overlapping terminology between three scientific disciplines
- Star Wars, the original three movies
